History

Ancient Origins

Go was born in China as Weiqi — 囲棋, the surrounding game — approximately 2500 to 3000 years before the common era. Chinese legend attributes it to Emperor Yao, who created it to develop in his son the qualities of discipline, concentration, and strategic foresight.

By the time of Confucius around 500 BC, Weiqi had already existed for centuries. It was listed among the Four Arts of the Scholar — alongside calligraphy, painting, and the guqin — marking it as a measure of educated civilization rather than mere entertainment.

Go reached Korea along trade routes, where it became Baduk, developing its own distinct professional tradition and culture. Japan adopted it in the 7th century AD, embedding it in Samurai culture and Zen Buddhist philosophy as Igo. In the Edo period, Japan formalized professional Go with four competing schools and government stipends for master players.

The three names — Weiqi, Baduk, Go — describe the same game. The English-speaking world adopted the Japanese name. Each tradition carries its own lineage, aesthetics, and style of play to this day.

Cultural weight

Go was never purely entertainment in East Asian civilization. Sun Tzu’s principles in The Art of War — encirclement, deception, patience, reading the terrain — map directly to Go strategy. Japanese Zen practitioners used Go to develop mushin: actionless action, presence without deliberation.

In South Korea, top professional players hold social status comparable to elite athletes. China’s national Go team trains under government support from childhood. Japan’s four major domestic titles carry combined prize funds exceeding ¥100 million annually. Go at its peak is simultaneously a career, a sport, a philosophy, and an art.

DeepMind

AlphaGo

When AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol 4–1 in March 2016, sixty million people across Asia watched live. Go grandmasters in the audience described its moves as alien — plays no human would consider, that revealed themselves as genius on analysis. The consensus that Go would resist AI for decades collapsed in a single week.

Go has approximately 10170 possible board positions — a number that exceeds the atoms in the observable universe by ninety orders of magnitude. No brute-force computation could ever address it. AlphaGo required something new: machines that learned to see the board rather than count positions.

The Lineage

October 2015
AlphaGo Fan
Defeated Fan Hui, European Champion, 5–0. First time a computer had beaten a professional Go player without handicap. Published in Nature. The Go world was skeptical — Fan Hui was not considered world class. The skepticism would not survive spring.
March 2016
AlphaGo Lee — The Cultural Earthquake
Defeated Lee Sedol — 18-time world champion — 4–1 in Seoul. Game 4, Move 78: Lee played a move that briefly dropped AlphaGo’s confidence from 70% to below 50%. It is the only game any human has won against a top-tier Go AI under competition conditions. Lee retired in 2019, citing the impossibility of ever defeating AI again.
January 2017
AlphaGo Master
Played anonymously online under the alias Master. Defeated every top player it faced — 60 consecutive games, 60 wins. Then defeated Ke Jie, world number one, 3–0 at the Future of Go Summit in Wuzhen. Ke Jie wept at the press conference. DeepMind announced AlphaGo would retire from competitive play.
October 2017
AlphaGo Zero — Trained From Nothing
No human game data. No human strategy. Started from random moves. Learned Go entirely through self-play in 40 days, then defeated AlphaGo Lee 100–0. Independently rediscovered joseki patterns humans had developed over centuries — then went beyond them into territory human theory had never mapped.
December 2017
AlphaZero — One Algorithm
The same architecture applied to Chess and Shogi with no domain-specific tuning. Mastered Chess in four hours, Shogi in two, Go in eight — all to superhuman level. A single general learning algorithm that defeated the best specialized programs ever built for each game separately.
2019
MuZero — Without Rules
AlphaZero without even being told the rules of the game. MuZero learned Go, Chess, Shogi, and 57 Atari games without being given any rule set. It built its own internal model of how games function, purely from experience. The machine that teaches itself what reality is.
Technical architecture

Policy Network — Trained on 30 million professional game moves. Learned which moves are worth considering, reducing the search tree from ~250 candidates to 2–5. The machine’s intuition about what matters.

Value Network — Outputs a single number from -1 to +1 estimating who is winning from any board position. Replaced expensive full-game simulations with a learned judgment.

Monte Carlo Tree Search — Explores future move sequences by running thousands of simulations. Guided by the policy network, evaluated by the value network. Balances exploration against exploitation.

Self-Play Reinforcement Learning — Millions of games played against itself. The version that wins more often survives. Evolutionary pressure applied to neural networks across time.

Intelligence

Why Go Defines AI

Chess fell to AI in 1997. Checkers was solved in 2007. Backgammon by 1992. Go resisted everything. The search space is 10120 orders of magnitude larger than Chess. Every computational approach that worked elsewhere failed catastrophically on 19×19 Go. AlphaGo required a fundamentally new kind of machine intelligence — one that learned to see rather than count.

The techniques we developed to play Go are now rewriting biology, physics, and chemistry. The board was always larger than the game.

Demis Hassabis — DeepMind founder
Technique
Monte Carlo Tree Search
Instead of evaluating every position — impossible in Go — MCTS samples thousands of complete game continuations and tracks which moves lead to wins more often. Combined with neural networks in AlphaGo, it became a general planning algorithm. Now used in robotics, protein design, drug discovery, materials science, and financial optimization.
Technique
Self-Play Reinforcement Learning
AlphaGo Zero’s training loop — play yourself, update on outcomes, repeat millions of times — is the direct ancestor of RLHF: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, used to train every major language model including ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. The insight that you do not need labeled examples if you can generate your own signal generalized from Go to language, code, and reasoning.
Technique
Policy + Value Network Architecture
AlphaGo’s dual-network design — one network for what moves to consider, one for who is winning — introduced a split between exploration and evaluation that appears throughout modern AI. In language models: the policy network maps to token selection, the value network maps to reward modeling. DeepMind cited this architecture explicitly in AlphaFold and materials discovery work.
2020–2026
Downstream Applications
AlphaFold solved protein structure prediction using policy+value architecture from Go. A 50-year unsolved biology problem. AlphaCode applies MCTS-style exploration to program space. AlphaDev discovered faster sorting algorithms by treating CPU instruction sequences as a Go-like game. GNoMe found 2.2 million new materials using Go-style self-play over molecular configurations. DeepMind plasma control for nuclear fusion uses the same reinforcement learning loop developed on the 9×9 board.

The branching gap

Chess averages ~35 legal moves per turn. Go averages ~250. In a 200-move Go game, the search tree contains approximately 250200 nodes. Chess’s 3580 tree is enormous but tractable with pruning. Go’s is simply beyond any exhaustive approach — which is why it required genuine machine intuition, not deeper calculation.

Destroy vs surround

Chess is about destruction — eliminating the enemy king. Go is about creation — surrounding territory. A Chess position can often be evaluated tactically by counting material. A Go position requires holistic judgment: influence, potential, thickness, life-and-death — concepts that resist numerical reduction. This is why Go demands something closer to aesthetic intuition.

Inside AlphaGo

How the AI Thinks

AlphaGo does not calculate every possible move. It imagines the most promising lines, evaluates them, and selects. Here is what happens inside the machine for every single move it makes — rendered live.

Policy Network — move heatmap
The policy network outputs a probability over all legal moves. Bright red = high probability, black = pruned. This reduces ~250 candidates to ~5, making deep search tractable.
Value Network — territory estimate
The value network outputs a single win-probability from 0–1 for any board position. Red = Black territory, green = White. One neural network pass replaces thousands of simulations.
Monte Carlo Tree Search — live simulation
MCTS builds a search tree by simulating promising continuations. Each node: visit count + win rate. Brighter = more explored. The best move = the child with the highest visit count after the simulation budget is spent.
Self-play training curve — AlphaGo Zero (40 days)
Win rate of each successive network generation against the starting random player. 29 million self-play games. 40 days. The curve you see is 40 days of machine evolution — from random to superhuman.
